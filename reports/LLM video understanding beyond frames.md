# Video LLMs learn to compress, search, and remember

Yes. Uniform frame sampling is now the baseline that new methods beat, not the state of the art. Five families of methods have replaced it, and each attacks a different bottleneck. **Token compression and pruning** cut each frame from roughly 144–729 tokens to 2–64, so a model can see hundreds to 10,000 frames. **Long context with time-aware position encodings and explicit timestamps** lets models place events in time over hours. **Query-aware frame selection and agentic search** look only where the question points. **Streaming memories** keep a fixed-size cache while the video plays. **The audio track** carries much of the answer in long videos. The payoff is concentrated in long video. On 3-minute clips, about 16 frames is nearly enough. On hour-long video, Gemini 1.5 Pro rose from **45.2% with 16 frames to 72.2% with every 1-fps frame** ([Gemini 1.5 report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v1_5_report.pdf)). On the hour-long LVBench, an agent built on o3 reaches **74.2%, 17.1 points above the same o3 shown 256 uniform frames** ([DVD](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18079v3)). Efficiency gains are real but smaller than the headlines: training-free pruners remove about 90% of video tokens while keeping 98–99% of accuracy, but 3–7× prefill speedups shrink to **1.3–1.9× end to end**. The gap to human performance is still large. The best hour-scale systems trail humans by about 20 points (LVBench: about 74–76 for the best agent vs about 94 for humans, per a [secondary summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/lvbench-dataset)). Gemini 3 Pro's 66.1% average on Video-MME-v2 falls to **49.4% once related questions must all be answered correctly; humans score 90.7%** ([Video-MME-v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05015v1)). Almost every number in this field is self-reported under different frame budgets, so cross-paper comparisons carry an error bar of several points.

## Naive sampling breaks at hour scale, not at three minutes

The case against "sample N frames uniformly and hope" depends on video length. Google's Gemini 1.5 ablation shows it most clearly. On EgoSchema (3-minute clips), accuracy rises from 53.6% with one frame to 70.2% with 16 frames, then barely moves to 72.7% at 150 frames. On 1H-VideoQA (40–105-minute videos), the same model goes from **23.8% (one frame) to 45.2% (16 frames), 56.3% (150 frames) and 72.2% (all frames at 1 fps)** ([Gemini 1.5 report, Fig. 15](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v1_5_report.pdf)). Sparse sampling is good enough for short clips and fails on long ones: a uniform sample of an hour simply misses the few seconds that contain the answer.

Dense frames are expensive, though. At Gemini's default rate of **263 tokens per second of video plus 32 per second of audio**, one hour costs about 1.06M tokens ([Gemini API token docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/tokens)). Gemini 1.5 actually used about 684K tokens for 2,674 frames of a 45-minute film ([Gemini 1.5 report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v1_5_report.pdf)). Open models face the same arithmetic. Qwen-VL's official preprocessing defaults to 2 fps, clamps each two-frame patch to 128–768 tokens, and caps input at 768 frames and a 128K budget, because uncapped settings would reach about 2.8M tokens per hour ([Qwen3-VL vision_process.py](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL/blob/main/qwen-vl-utils/src/qwen_vl_utils/vision_process.py)). As a back-of-envelope estimate (assuming a Qwen2-7B-class layout of 28 layers, 4 KV heads and head dimension 128 in BF16, which no retrieved source states), 1M tokens of KV cache is roughly 57 GB per sequence before weights, and prefill attention grows quadratically. Every method below either spends fewer tokens per second of video, spends them only where the question needs them, or stops holding all of them at once.

A second problem makes "better" harder to measure: popular benchmarks reward much less temporal understanding than their names suggest. Shuffling frames barely changes accuracy on MVBench, but drives TVBench to near chance, and reversing frames pushes even the best models below random ([Lost in Time / TVBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.07752)). A text-only Flan-T5-xl scores **35.9% on EgoSchema against 20% chance** ([Koala](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.04346)). On counterfactual pairs that swap event order, GPT-4o manages about 50% against about 90% for humans ([Vinoground](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.02763v1)). On HourVideo, Gemini 1.5 Pro scored **37.3% against 85.0% for human experts** ([HourVideo](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.04998v1)). This report therefore weights hour-scale results (LVBench, 1H-VideoQA, the Video-MME long split) and temporally strict tests more heavily than saturated short-clip benchmarks.

A note on evidence quality. The research behind this report could not reach arXiv, Hugging Face or the live leaderboards, so many figures come from GitHub READMEs, conference PDFs, technical reports and search summaries. Unless marked otherwise, results are **self-reported by the method's authors**. Where sources disagree, both values are given. The conflicts that matter most are on LVBench. Gemini 2.5 Pro is reported at 78.7 in Google's report and 67.4 in the LVBench paper's own table ([Gemini 2.5 report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v2_5_report.pdf); [LVBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08035v3)). GPT-4o is listed at 30.8 in one vendor table and 66.7 in another ([Qwen3-Omni README](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-Omni); [Eagle 2.5 README](https://github.com/NVlabs/EAGLE/blob/main/Eagle2_5/README.md)). LVBench numbers depend heavily on protocol, so they should be compared only within a single table.

## Compression turns 729 tokens per frame into 16, and buys 10× more frames

The most widely adopted alternative to naive sampling keeps dense sampling but makes each frame cheap. The 2023–24 pioneers pushed this to extremes: LLaMA-VID represents each frame with **two tokens** (one context, one content) to reach hour-long video ([LLaMA-VID](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dvlab-research/LLaMA-VID/main/README.md)). PLLaVA showed that pooling space while keeping time beats pooling time ([PLLaVA](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/magic-research/PLLaVA/main/README.md)). The 2025 generation learned that *where* compression happens matters as much as how much. NVIDIA's STORM inserts a **Mamba temporal encoder between the image encoder and the LLM**, so each token already carries motion context before frames are averaged away. It reports more than 5% gains on MLVU and LongVideoBench, up to 8× less computation, and 2.4–2.9× lower decoding latency ([STORM](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04130v2)). BIMBA's bidirectional selective scan makes the mechanism explicit: **pooling-based compression saturates and even declines beyond 16 frames, while selective-scan compression keeps improving as frames are added** ([BIMBA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.09590)). Shanghai AI Lab's hierarchical compression (HiCo) takes a similar mix-then-compress approach. At 16 tokens per frame, InternVideo2.5 reports Video-MME 65.1, MLVU 72.8, LongVideoBench 60.6 and LVBench 46.4 ([InternVideo2.5 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenGVLab/InternVideo/main/InternVideo2.5/README.md)). Its sibling VideoChat-Flash handles **10,000 frames (about 3 hours) with 99.1% needle-in-a-haystack accuracy** ([VideoChat-Flash README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenGVLab/VideoChat-Flash/main/README.md)).

The same idea appears in other forms. Meta's LongVU uses DINOv2 similarity to drop near-duplicate frames, applies a text-guided query to shrink the rest, and averages about **2 tokens per frame at 1 fps, enough to fit an hour into an 8K context** ([LongVU](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.17434)). Apple's SlowFast-LLaVA-1.5 gives a few frames many tokens and many frames few tokens. Its slow-fast design uses about **30% of the tokens of a slow-only baseline (9K vs 28K) while scoring higher**, and the 7B model reaches LongVideoBench 62.5 and MLVU 71.5 ([SlowFast-LLaVA-1.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.18943)). Keye-VL 1.5 adopts the same split: changed frames go through a high-resolution "slow" path and static frames through a low-resolution "fast" path ([Keye-VL 1.5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01563)). BAAI's Video-XL-2 compresses the KV cache rather than the input. It uses chunked prefill with full attention inside a chunk and sparse attention across chunks, then reloads dense KV only for query-relevant chunks at decode time. This cuts prefill FLOPs to 48.8% and KV cache by 38.8% with under 0.5 points of accuracy loss ([Video-XL-2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.19225)). Its reported MLVU 74.9, Video-MME 66.4 and LVBench 48.6 come only from an unofficial project page ([Video-XL-2 project page](https://unabletousegit.github.io/video-xl2.github.io/)). Frontier labs do the same: Google trained Gemini 2.5 to perform competitively with **66 instead of 258 tokens per frame, fitting about 3 hours rather than 1 into 1M tokens** ([Gemini 2.5 report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v2_5_report.pdf)). The developer blog quotes 84.7% vs 85.2% on Video-MME for low vs default resolution ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/gemini-2-5-video-understanding/)).

These trained compressors converge on similar numbers. **7B models at 16–64 tokens per frame land at about Video-MME 65–66, MLVU 71–75 and LVBench 45–49**, which suggests that compression design alone has hit diminishing returns at this scale.

### Training-free pruning removes 90% of tokens, but end-to-end speedups are modest

A separate line of work prunes or merges tokens at inference time in an existing model, with no retraining. The table summarizes the strongest reported results. All are self-reported, mostly on LLaVA-OneVision-7B.

| Method | Mechanism | Tokens removed | Accuracy retained | Speed / compute |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FastVID ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11187)) | Temporal segmentation, then density-peak pruning per segment | 90.3% | 98.0% | FLOPs to 8.3%; prefill 7.1× faster |
| HoliTom ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cokeshao/HoliTom/main/README.md)) | Global temporal segmentation and merging outside the LLM, similarity merging inside | >90% | 99.1% | FLOPs to 6.9%; TTFT 2.28×; decode 1.32× |
| FrameFusion ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/thu-nics/FrameFusion/main/README.md)) | Similarity merging plus importance pruning | 70% | "minimal impact" | LLM 3.4–4.4×; **end to end 1.6–1.9×** |
| PruneVid ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Visual-AI/PruneVid/main/README.md)) | Spatio-temporal merging plus question-guided selection | 83–85% | within ~1 point | FLOPs 0.20–0.26× |
| VisionZip ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dvlab-research/VisionZip/main/README.md)) | Text-agnostic dominant plus contextual tokens | ~90% | ~95% | 32 frames at the cost of 16 |
| DyCoke ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/KD-TAO/DyCoke/main/README.md)) | Temporal merging plus dynamic KV pruning while decoding | — | slight gain | 1.5× faster; 1.4× less memory |

Two patterns stand out. First, pruning guided by visual cues and temporal redundancy holds up much better than early text-attention pruning. At 94.4% reduction on Video-LLaVA, VisPruner keeps 90.3% of accuracy against FastV's 86% ([VisPruner](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.01818)). In an independent evaluation on LLaVA-Video at 16 tokens per frame, FastV falls to 78.6% retention while TOPS keeps 92.3% ([TOPS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.27161)). Second, the user-visible speedup is far smaller than the prefill number, because the vision encoder and decoding still run at full cost. No independent group has compared the methods in the table head to head on one model. One independent study found that a published pruning method did not work as described: VideoLLaMA3's differential frame pruning **removes almost no tokens in practice**, with a keep ratio near 1 ([long-video efficiency study](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.04318v1)).

### Codec-aware models reuse the motion vectors the encoder already computed

An emerging 2025–26 branch skips decoding redundant frames entirely. These models give I-frames full RGB tokens and represent P/B-frames with cheap **motion-vector and residual tokens** taken from the video codec. EMA groups video by GOP (group of pictures) and fuses each I-frame with sampled motion vectors. It reportedly uses 648 tokens and 127 ms, against Video-LLaVA's 2,048 tokens and 391 ms ([EMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13016); figures via [secondary review](https://liner.com/review/efficient-motionaware-video-mllm)). CoPE-VideoLM claims better accuracy at **19.5% of the tokens and up to 86% lower time-to-first-token** ([CoPE-VideoLM](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13191v2)). AdaCodec places I-frames adaptively and reports gains over per-frame Qwen3-VL-8B at a matched token budget ([AdaCodec](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.02569v1)). The appeal is clear: the codec has already done motion estimation, so finding redundancy is nearly free. The catch is that block motion vectors are noisy, which is why ReMoRa refines them ([ReMoRa](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16412v1)). So far none of these models reports top-tier long-video scores, so they are an efficiency bet rather than a proven accuracy gain.

### More frames with fewer tokens wins, up to a point

The central design question is how to spend a fixed token budget, and the evidence favors more frames at lower fidelity, within limits. A 2026 controlled study finds **16 compressed frames beat 8 full-resolution frames at equal or lower cost by +2.24 on LongVideoBench**. At a fixed total budget, halving tokens per frame once (1024 to 512) lifts accuracy from 59.76 to 61.03, but halving again (256) drops it to 59.31 ([Select, Compress, Reinvest](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03820)). HiCo shows that the right per-frame count depends on the task. Going from 16 to 64 tokens per frame adds **+3.1 on LongVideoBench and +1.5 on Video-MME but only +0.4 on MVBench**, at four times the cost ([InternVideo2.5 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/OpenGVLab/InternVideo/main/InternVideo2.5/README.md)). LongVU finds 144 tokens per frame beats 64 on Video-MME and MLVU but loses on EgoSchema ([LongVU](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.17434)). Under extreme scarcity (4K–8K tokens), forcing 128 frames causes over-compression, and 64 frames wins ([VideoRouter](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.05848)). Apollo's design-space study adds that **sampling at a fixed fps beats uniform sampling** in both training and inference ([Apollo](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Zohar_Apollo__An_Exploration_of_Video_Understanding_in_Large_Multimodal_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)). The practical optimum for 7B open models is about 16–64 tokens per frame at roughly 1 fps, ideally with temporal mixing before compression and a query-adaptive allocation rather than a fixed point on the curve.

### Native video encoders improve each token, not the token count

Self-supervised video encoders produce better motion features per token. Meta's V-JEPA 2 scores **77.3 on Something-Something v2 against 69.7 for InternVideo2-1B**. Aligned to an 8B LLM, it reports PerceptionTest 84.0 and TempCompass 76.9 ([V-JEPA 2 README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/main/README.md)). Meta's PerceptionLM-8B reaches MVBench 77.1 but only 58.3 on Video-MME ([PLM README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/perception_models/main/README.md)). Strong short-clip perception does not by itself buy long-video ability; the token budget still has to be managed. Discrete video tokenizers built for generation (MAGVIT-v2, Cosmos) compress aggressively, but the literature consistently reports that they **trade away understanding accuracy** compared with continuous features ([Divot](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Ge_Divot_Diffusion_Powers_Video_Tokenizer_for_Comprehension_and_Generation_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf); [discrete tokenization survey](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.22920v2)).

## Time needs its own coordinates: long context, position encodings and audio

The second family brute-forces length while fixing how the model represents time. LongVA showed that long-context ability **transfers zero-shot from text to vision**. Extending Qwen2-7B's text context to 224K (about two days on 8×A100) lets the model process 2,000 frames or more than 200K visual tokens with no long-video training ([LongVA](https://github.com/EvolvingLMMs-Lab/LongVA)). Its Video-MME score still peaked at 128 frames (52.6) and dipped at 384 ([LongVA paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16852)). NVIDIA's LongVILA built multimodal sequence parallelism to train at 2M context on 256 GPUs. It reports **99.8% needle accuracy in a 6,000-frame (over 1M-token) video**, but only 65.1% on Video-MME with subtitles per its README, or 61.8% per the arXiv abstract ([LongVILA README](https://github.com/NVlabs/VILA/blob/main/longvila/README.md); [LongVILA arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.10188v6)). That gap shows that single-needle retrieval is essentially solved and does not predict QA quality. Eagle 2.5-8B instead uses a moderate 128K context with sampling that keeps all text and fills the rest with frames. It reports **Video-MME 72.4 (75.7 with subtitles) at 512 frames, LVBench 66.4 and HourVideo 44.5/41.8**, the last above Gemini 1.5 Pro's 37.2/37.4 in the same table ([Eagle 2.5 README](https://github.com/NVlabs/EAGLE/blob/main/Eagle2_5/README.md)). Kuaishou's Keye-VL-2.0 adds sparse attention and reports accuracy that **keeps rising from 64 to 512 frames on Video-MME-v2 (35.3% to 42.4%)**, while most competitors degrade with more frames ([Keye README](https://github.com/Kwai-Keye/Keye)).

At the frontier, Gemini 2.5 Pro reports **81.0 on 1H-VideoQA with 7,200 frames, 84.3 on the Video-MME long split with audio, and 86.9 on full Video-MME with audio and subtitles**. In the same table, GPT-4.1, capped at 500 frames by its API, scores 56.8 on 1H-VideoQA ([Gemini 2.5 report, Table 6](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/gemini/gemini_v2_5_report.pdf)). Qwen3-VL brings the open frontier close: native 256K context expandable to 1M, evaluation at up to 2,048 frames within 224K video tokens, and **Video-MME 79.0, MLVU 83.8 and LVBench 63.6 for the 235B model** ([Qwen3-VL README](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL); [Qwen3-VL report](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631v2/); [benchmark gist](https://gist.github.com/karminski/aa56b5b73e2f3070a7530dfe11c2ddb3)). Aggregators list Gemini 3 Pro at 87.7 and GPT-5.2 at 85.8 on Video-MME without subtitles, but these are third-party re-listings not checked against vendor pages ([BenchLM](https://benchlm.ai/benchmarks/videoMmeNoSub)).

### Position encodings moved time to low frequencies, then to text timestamps

Feeding many frames only helps if the model can tell where each one sits in time. The standard rotary scheme was not built for this. Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE splits rotary dimensions into time, height and width. Qwen2.5-VL then aligned temporal IDs to **absolute time**, so the model learns pace and can localize events to the second ([Qwen2.5-VL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13923)). Qwen3-VL's report rejects that choice: absolute-time IDs become "excessively large and sparse" on long videos. Qwen3-VL instead prefixes each temporal patch with a **text timestamp such as "<3.0 seconds>"** and interleaves time, height and width across all rotary frequencies. Because its position IDs grow more slowly, a smaller YaRN factor (2–3) reaches 1M tokens ([Qwen3-VL README](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL); [Qwen3-VL report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.21631)). Academic work points the same way. VideoRoPE puts time on low frequencies because high-frequency temporal channels oscillate and are fooled by periodic look-alike distractors. It gains **+12.4 on the distractor needle test V-NIAH-D**, but only +2.9 on LongVideoBench, +4.5 on MLVU and +1.7 on Video-MME at 64K context ([VideoRoPE](https://github.com/Wiselnn570/VideoRoPE)). HoPE drops position encoding for time on the lowest frequencies. Trained at 8K and tested at 64K on a 2B model, it lifts **LongVideoBench from 34.21 to 46.27**, with no difference at the training length ([HoPE](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.20444)). V2PE uses fractional position increments (1/256) for visual tokens to stretch a 2B model to 1M tokens ([V2PE](https://github.com/OpenGVLab/V2PE)). The lesson is consistent: position-encoding fixes matter most when **extrapolating beyond the training length and for retrieval**, and add only a few points on standard QA.

Explicit time also fixes a specific failure in temporal grounding: models often attend to the right segment but cannot turn it into accurate boundary timestamps in text ([Number-It supplementary](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/supplemental/Wu_Number_it_Temporal_CVPR_2025_supplemental.pdf)). Number-It's training-free fix is to **print the frame number on each frame**. This matches timestamps on short Charades-STA videos and beats them on the longer ActivityNet videos ([Number-It](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Wu_Number_it_Temporal_Grounding_Videos_like_Flipping_Manga_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)). With GRPO and an IoU reward, fine-tuned 7B grounding models reach about 72 R1@0.5 on Charades-STA (Time-R1 72.2) and about 61 mIoU (TAR 61.1). The generalist Qwen3-VL-235B now reports 63.5 mIoU ([Time-R1](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/7801b29c93b599b8d0c44138596bdeed-Paper-Conference.pdf); [TAR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07683); [benchmark gist](https://gist.github.com/karminski/aa56b5b73e2f3070a7530dfe11c2ddb3)).

### Listening is the cheapest long-video upgrade

Speech carries much of the answer in long videos, and using it costs far fewer tokens than more frames. On Video-MME, where every long video has subtitles, **adding subtitles lifts most models by 3–8 points overall**:

| Model | No subtitles | With subtitles | Gain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gemini 1.5 Pro | 75.0 | 81.3 | +6.3 |
| GPT-4o (384 frames) | 71.9 | 77.2 | +5.3 |
| Eagle 2.5-8B (512 frames) | 72.4 | 75.7 | +3.3 |
| Qwen2.5-VL-7B | 65.1 | 71.6 | +6.5 |
| Qwen2.5-Omni-7B | 64.3 | 72.4 | +8.1 |

Sources: [Eagle 2.5 README](https://github.com/NVlabs/EAGLE/blob/main/Eagle2_5/README.md); [Qwen2.5-Omni README](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen2.5-Omni).

The gain grows with length. Two versions of the Video-MME paper disagree on the size for Gemini 1.5 Pro on long videos. One reports +10.1 for subtitles and +6.2 for audio. The CVPR version reports **+16.7 for subtitles and +12.5 for audio** ([Video-MME CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Fu_Video-MME_The_First-Ever_Comprehensive_Evaluation_Benchmark_of_Multi-modal_LLMs_in_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)). Both put the gain well above the overall figure. Native audio-visual models now substitute raw audio for subtitles. video-SALMONN 2 Pro (32B, on a Qwen3-VL backbone) reports **82.7 on Video-MME with audio input** ([video-SALMONN 2 README](https://github.com/bytedance/video-SALMONN-2)). Omni models are costly to self-host, however. The reference implementation of Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B needs **145 GB for a 2-minute clip** ([Qwen3-Omni README](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-Omni)). Raw audio can also hurt weaker models: on OmniEval it **halved MiniCPM-o 2.6's score (54.88 to 27.79)**, while subtitles helped every model tested ([OmniEval](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.20960)). A practical corollary: part of any "long-video" score measures transcript understanding, so frame-only scores on the long split are the cleaner test of visual long-context ability.

## Looking where the question points beats looking everywhere

The third family stops treating the video as a fixed input and lets the question decide what to look at. The cheapest form is **query-aware keyframe selection**: score densely sampled candidate frames against the question with a CLIP- or SigLIP-style model, then pass the best subset to an unchanged Video-LLM. Methods that balance relevance with coverage or diversity beat naive top-k, which tends to pick clusters of near-identical neighbors.

| Method | Mechanism | Backbone, budget | Reported gain over uniform |
|---|---|---|---|
| AKS ([CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Tang_Adaptive_Keyframe_Sampling_for_Long_Video_Understanding_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)) | Relevance vs coverage via recursive partitioning | Qwen2-VL / LLaVA-Video-7B | LongVideoBench +5.0 / +3.8; Video-MME +2.3 / +0.9 |
| BOLT ([CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Liu_BOLT_Boost_Large_Vision-Language_Model_Without_Training_for_Long-form_Video_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)) | Inverse-transform sampling over similarity scores | LLaVA-OV-7B, 8 frames | Video-MME 53.8→56.1; MLVU 58.9→63.4 |
| MDP3 ([ICCV 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Sun_MDP3_A_Training-free_Approach_for_List-wise_Frame_Selection_in_Video-LLMs_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf)) | List-wise relevance, diversity and order | VILA-1.5, 8 frames | Video-MME 47.5→53.3; MLVU 46.3→58.6 |
| Q-Frame ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.22139)) | Gumbel-Max selection plus higher resolution for relevant frames | VILA-1.5, 8 frames | Video-MME 47.5→50.7; MLVU 46.3→54.4 |
| TSPO ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.04369v2)) | RL-trained sampling policy in front of a frozen model | LLaVA-Video-7B | LongVideoBench +5.0; MLVU +6.0; LVBench +5.1; Video-MME +1.1 |

The pattern across papers is consistent. **The weaker the backbone and the smaller the budget, the bigger the gain.** VILA-1.5 at 8 frames gains 8–12 points on MLVU, while strong 7B models at 32–64 frames gain about 1–2 points on Video-MME. Video-MME's holistic questions suit uniform coverage, while the "needle"-style questions in MLVU and LongVideoBench reward targeted selection. Selection adds one cheap scoring pass and keeps a single LLM call. A complementary trick retrieves text instead of frames. Video-RAG adds about 2K tokens of ASR, OCR and object-detection text per query and reports **+8.0% averaged across six LVLMs** ([Video-RAG](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.13093v3)).

### Agents turn video QA into search, and lead on hour-long video

Agentic systems go further. They index the video once, then let a reasoning LLM search it iteratively. The first wave (2024) used GPT-4 over captions. VideoAgent looked at only **about 8 frames per question and reached 54.1% on full EgoSchema** ([VideoAgent](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.10517)). VideoTree's query-guided hierarchical clustering reached 61.1% and hit 63.6% in 7.8 seconds, about a third of the best caption-everything baseline's time ([VideoTree](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.19209)). The 2025 wave targets hour-long LVBench:

| System | Approach | Reasoner | LVBench |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deep Video Discovery ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18079)) | 5-s clip database; Global Browse, Clip Search, Frame Inspect tools | o3 | **74.2** (76.0 with transcripts) |
| Gemini 2.5 Pro, single model ([LVBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.08035v3)) | Long context | — | 67.4 (78.7 self-reported) |
| Eagle 2.5-8B, single model ([README](https://github.com/NVlabs/EAGLE/blob/main/Eagle2_5/README.md)) | 512 frames, 128K context | — | 66.4 |
| MR. Video ([NeurIPS 2025](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/0a02c2bc2e2148b803c4ade1d71e1d25-Paper-Conference.pdf)) | MapReduce: caption clips in parallel, aggregate | Gemini-2.0-Flash | 60.8 |
| VideoLucy ([paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.12422)) | Coarse-to-fine hierarchical memory with backtracking | — | 58.8 |
| TimeSearch ([paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01407v1)) | Training-free coarse-to-fine temporal search | — | 51.5 (from 41.8) |

The strongest causal evidence comes from DVD's own ablation: the full agent **beats o3 viewing 256 uniform frames by 17.1 points** ([DVD](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18079v3)). That gain comes from search and inspection, not from a better model. DVD's design is also the template for the strongest hybrids. Text indexes (captions, subtitles) are used to *find* moments, and pixels are used to *verify* them. Pure caption pipelines lose counting, fine spatial detail and precise ordering, and per-frame captions drop temporal context ([VideoAgent2](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/124650)).

Agents have well-documented failure modes. **Accuracy collapses with a weaker reasoner.** Swapping o3 for o4-mini or GPT-4o makes DVD drop sharply, and GPT-4o stops searching too early out of "overconfidence" ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/articles/deep-video-discovery-using-agentic-search-to-analyze-long-form-video/)). Tool errors compound: a misdetected object or wrong caption corrupts the retrieval plan, and errors accumulate step by step ([VideoAgent2](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2025/124650)). Cost is also undisclosed. DVD requires an offline captioning-and-embedding pass over every 5-second clip, and no paper reports dollars or wall-clock time per LVBench question. Agent comparison tables also often mix leaderboard and paper numbers, so they are not fully like-for-like ([Liner review of MR. Video](https://liner.com/review/mr-video-mapreduce-is-principle-for-long-video-understanding)).

### RL-trained reasoning helps verifiable tasks; long chains of thought drift from the pixels

Since March 2025, GRPO-style RL on Qwen2.5-VL-7B has become the default open recipe. Video-R1's T-GRPO adds a reward that pays only when the model answers better with ordered frames than with shuffled ones, explicitly rewarding use of temporal order. Replacing it with vanilla GRPO cuts VSI-Bench from **34.6 to 32.7** ([Video-R1](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.21776v3)). Typical gains over the base model are moderate: VideoRFT reports **+5.0 VSI-Bench, +7.2 MMVU and +7.0 Video-MME** ([VideoRFT](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.12434)). Narrow, verifiable tasks gain more: SpaceR reaches **45.6 on VSI-Bench, 11.6 above GPT-4o** ([SpaceR](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01805)). On long video, plain RL adds little. LongVILA-R1-7B's 58.0 on LongVideoBench is about 0.3 points above LongVILA-7B ([LongVILA-R1](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.07966); [VideoExplorer table](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.10821)). Teaching the model to *act on the video* works better. FrameThinker learns to request frames iteratively and reports **76.1% on LongVideo-Reason with 20.6 frames on average, against LongVILA-R1's 72.0% with many more frames** ([FrameThinker](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.24304)). Small RL agents need a fair baseline, though. Video-MTR's 32-frame results sit below Qwen2.5-VL-7B's own numbers at its native frame budget ([Video-MTR review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/video-mtr-reinforced-multi-turn-reasoning-for-long-video-understanding); [Qwen2.5-VL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13923)).

On whether explicit chain-of-thought helps, the evidence leans skeptical for perception-heavy video. Extended CoT "provides little benefit on existing video understanding benchmarks" and is essential only on benchmarks built for multi-step visual reasoning ([VideoReasonBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.23359)). Long reasoning traces tend to become verbose and ungrounded ([When Thinking Drifts](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2510.06077)). Models also forget visual evidence, a problem one paper mitigates by re-injecting frames ([When Thinking Hurts](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.16256)). Reasoning helps when the evidence has already been perceived and must be combined. It does not repair perception.

## Streaming models remember instead of re-reading

The fourth family serves video that never ends: live feeds, robots, glasses, long recordings. These models cannot sample frames up front because the frames do not exist yet. The early designs merged tokens into a fixed memory bank. The 2025 shift is to **training-free KV-cache memories** bolted onto strong offline models, and they split into two camps. **Query-aware** memories keep everything and retrieve on demand. ReKV encodes the stream with sliding-window attention, offloads the KV cache to CPU RAM or disk, and loads only question-relevant blocks back onto the GPU, trading host memory for recall ([ReKV](https://github.com/becomebright/rekv)). **Query-agnostic** memories enforce a hard cap. InfiniPot-V drops tokens that repeat earlier frames and keeps those with high value norms. It reports **up to 94% lower peak GPU memory, 14 fps real-time generation, and a 50K-token context cut to about 6K at near-full accuracy** ([InfiniPot-V](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.15745)). StreamMem beats the full cache at a 24K budget ([StreamMem](https://agenticlearning.ai/research/stream-mem/paper.pdf)). The trade-off is clear. Capped memories may irreversibly discard a detail a later question needs. Offloading preserves it at a storage cost that grows with stream length.

Two simpler mechanisms are effective. TimeChat-Online's Differential Token Drop removes patches that barely change between consecutive frames, and finds **80%+ of streaming visual tokens are redundant**. Applied zero-shot to Qwen2.5-VL-7B, it improves the Video-MME long split by **+5.7 while using 84.6% fewer tokens** ([TimeChat-Online](https://github.com/yaolinli/TimeChat-Online)). Its StreamingBench score is reported as 56.6 in the README and 58.0 elsewhere ([alphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2504.17343)). MIT and NVIDIA's StreamingVLM keeps attention-sink states, a short window of recent vision tokens and a longer window of recent text. It sustains **8 fps on one H100 over 2+ hour streams** with a 66.18% win rate against GPT-4o mini on commentary ([StreamingVLM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.09608)). Flash-VStream sets a practical latency rule of thumb: about **12K or fewer visual tokens for sub-second responses** on an A100 ([Flash-VStream ICCV 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Zhang_Flash-VStream_Efficient_Real-Time_Understanding_for_Long_Video_Streams_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf)).

Deciding *when* to speak is the weakest link. VideoLLM-online predicts a per-frame "stay silent" token ([VideoLLM-online](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Chen_VideoLLM-online_Online_Video_Large_Language_Model_for_Streaming_Video_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)). The 2025 consensus decouples that decision from answering. Dispider uses an asynchronous decision module, so video processing continues while the model answers ([Dispider](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.03218v1)). Apple's StreamBridge uses a separate 0.5B activation model plus round-decayed memory compression ([StreamBridge](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/bf6939f9058a391c47014731b2486e2a-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Proactive scores remain low. Dispider's 25.3 on StreamingBench's Proactive Output task is among the best cited ([Moonlight review](https://www.themoonlight.io/en/review/dispider-enabling-video-llms-with-active-real-time-interaction-via-disentangled-perception-decision-and-reaction)). Even Gemini 1.5 Pro scores only 35.5 on OVO-Bench's forward-looking REC task, against 69.3 on real-time perception ([OVO-Bench](https://github.com/JoeLeelyf/OVO-Bench)). RTV-Bench finds that **larger models or higher frame rates do not significantly help** real-time tasks ([RTV-Bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.02064)). Streaming leaderboard numbers are especially messy: real-time subsets get quoted as overall scores, and benchmark code has been revised since release. Quote the subset alongside the number. For production, Qwen3-Omni reports about **0.5 s first-packet latency for audio-video interaction** ([Qwen blog](https://qwen.ai/blog?id=fdfbaf2907a36b7659a470c77fb135e381302028&from=research.research-list)).

## Choosing a method by video length, latency budget and who hosts the model

The research supports a decision rule based on video length and deployment constraints. The table translates the evidence into starting points. Accuracy ranges are self-reported and indicative.

| Scenario | Recommended approach | Why / expected payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Clips under ~3 minutes | Strong base model (Qwen2.5/3-VL, InternVL3.5-class) with fps sampling at 1–2 fps; spend budget on resolution, not frames | EgoSchema-type tasks saturate at ~16 frames; detail and OCR need more tokens per frame |
| 10–60 min, self-hosted, offline QA | Compressed-token model (16–64 tokens/frame, e.g. InternVideo2.5, Video-XL-2, Eagle 2.5, Qwen3-VL with token caps), plus training-free keyframe selection (AKS, BOLT, MDP3) and ASR/OCR text (Video-RAG style) | 7B models reach ~65–72 Video-MME; selection adds +1–5 on needle-style questions; transcripts add +3–8 |
| Throughput-bound serving of an existing model | Training-free pruning (HoliTom, FastVID, FrameFusion); reinvest savings into more frames | ~90% fewer tokens at ~98–99% retention, but plan for **1.3–1.9× end-to-end** speedups, not 7× |
| Hour-plus or multi-hour archives, accuracy first | Agentic index-and-search (DVD pattern: 5-s clip captions plus subtitles for retrieval, frame inspection for verification) with a top reasoning model | Best LVBench (74–76), +17 over the same model with uniform frames; needs an offline index, and fails with weak reasoners |
| Hour-plus, simplicity first | Frontier long-context API at low media resolution (66–70 tokens/frame) with audio on | About 0.35M tokens per hour including audio (≈3 h per 1M); Gemini reports ~0.5-point loss vs default resolution |
| Live streams and assistants | KV-cache memory (InfiniPot-V/StreamMem for hard caps; ReKV if distant recall matters), redundancy dropping (DTD), sink-plus-window for commentary, separate small trigger model | Constant memory at 8–14 fps on one GPU; sub-second replies need ≤~12K visual tokens |
| Temporal grounding / timestamps | Timestamp-aware model (Qwen3-VL text timestamps) or frame-number overlays (Number-It) without training; GRPO with an IoU reward if fine-tuning | ~60–63 mIoU on Charades-STA from both generalist and RL-tuned models |
| Fine-tuning for reasoning | RL with verifiable rewards, including an order-sensitivity reward like T-GRPO; prefer "act on the video" (request frames, zoom) over longer text chains | +2–7 on reasoning benchmarks; little gain on perception; long CoT drifts |

The token arithmetic determines cost on any provider. Gemini 3 bills general video at **70 tokens per frame at low and medium resolution, and 280 at high resolution for text-heavy video**, plus 25 tokens per second of audio ([Gemini media resolution docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/media-resolution)). At 1 fps, low resolution costs about 0.34M tokens per hour including audio, against about 1.05M at Gemini 2.5's 258-token default. Current per-token prices were not verifiable for this report, so multiply these counts by your vendor's input price. Self-hosting at 1M tokens is technically demonstrated (LongVILA-7B-1M, V2PE, Qwen3-VL with YaRN), but it takes multi-GPU sequence parallelism, and quality at those lengths lags moderate-context models. The cost-effective open-model recipe is a **128–256K context with adaptive sampling and sparse or compressed KV**, not dense 1M.

Evaluation deserves equal care, because the wrong benchmark makes every method look good. Report the **Video-MME long split without subtitles** and LVBench alongside overall Video-MME. Fix frame counts and subtitle use when comparing. Add a frame-shuffle or reversal control before claiming temporal gains (TVBench showed MVBench barely notices shuffling). Use grouped-consistency scoring (Video-MME-v2) or counterfactual pairs (Vinoground) for robustness. Treat single-needle retrieval as a sanity check only. LongVILA hits 99.8% on a 6,000-frame needle yet scores in the low-to-mid 60s on Video-MME with subtitles.

## Conclusion

The field's main lesson is that "watching" video is a budgeting problem: what to spend tokens on and when. Frame count matters less. Every successful approach redistributes a fixed budget. Compression spreads it thinly across many frames. Selection and agents concentrate it where the question points. Memories spread it over time. Audio replaces expensive pixels with cheap words. The hybrids that win combine these moves rather than choosing one. DVD's text-search-then-pixel-verify loop and Video-XL-2's dense-KV-for-relevant-chunks are the clearest examples. One implication is that progress on hour-scale understanding is now gated as much by reasoner calibration (knowing when to keep looking) and by temporal position design as by raw perception. That is why the same o3 gains 17 points by searching the video rather than by seeing more of it.

The second implication is less comfortable. Much of the measured progress sits on benchmarks that partly reward language priors, single frames and transcripts. On tests that strictly require temporal consistency, frontier models still score in the 40s and 50s against humans near 90. The next real advance will likely come from methods that are trained and evaluated against order-sensitive, hour-scale and group-consistent targets, such as T-GRPO's shuffle reward, VideoRoPE's distractor tests and Video-MME-v2's grouped scoring, rather than from another 16→64 tokens-per-frame tweak. Until independent, matched-budget evaluations become the norm, practitioners should read self-reported gains under about 2 points as noise and judge methods by their gains on long, frame-only, temporally controlled splits.
